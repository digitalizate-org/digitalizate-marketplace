## Why
Bulleted and numbered lists are one of the most powerful tools for web readability because they exploit multiple psychological mechanisms simultaneously: they force chunking (each item is a discrete unit), they create vertical whitespace between items (visual separation), they remove prose connectives (words like 'and', 'also', 'furthermore') that slow scanning, and they signal parallelism (all items share equal visual weight). NNGroup's 1997 study found scannable layout (including the use of lists) improved measured usability by 47%. Ordered lists (numbered) communicate sequence or priority; unordered lists (bulleted) communicate equivalence.

## How to implement
- Convert any sentence containing 3 or more items joined by commas into a bulleted list
- Use numbered lists exclusively for sequential steps, ranked priorities, or ordered procedures — where the order matters
- Keep each list item to 1–2 lines; longer items defeat the scanning benefit and should become separate paragraphs with subheadings
- Ensure grammatical parallelism within a list: all items should start with the same grammatical form (all verbs, all nouns, all phrases)
- Introduce every list with a stem sentence that contextualizes the list and ends with a colon
- Do not nest lists more than 2 levels deep; deeper nesting signals a need to restructure content
- Avoid using lists for fewer than 3 items — two-item lists often read more naturally as a sentence

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Completion rate
- **Efficiency**: Time on task
- **Engagement & Usage**: Scroll depth, Bounce rate
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: Ease-of-use score, Satisfaction rating
**How to measure**: A/B test instructional or informational content with scannable bullet/numbered lists versus dense prose; measure comprehension accuracy in reading tests, time-to-find specific information, and scroll depth to confirm improved scannability.
