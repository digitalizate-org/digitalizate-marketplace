## Why
Psychologist Hermann Ebbinghaus demonstrated that people remember items at the beginning (primacy effect) and end (recency effect) of a list or sequence far better than items in the middle. The primacy effect operates through long-term memory encoding — early items receive more rehearsal. The recency effect uses short-term working memory — the last items are still active when recall happens. In web design, this means that navigation items, feature lists, pricing tiers, and onboarding steps all suffer from a 'forgettable middle' where the most important content can be buried.

## How to implement
- Place the most important navigation items first (primary destination) and last (key utility like settings or logout) in menu structures
- In feature comparison tables and pricing pages, lead with your strongest value point and end with a memorable differentiator; move average features to the middle
- In multi-step forms and onboarding flows, put the most cognitively demanding step in the middle (primacy captures motivation, recency captures completion satisfaction)
- In bulleted lists, front-load the most critical item and put the second-most critical last; pad the middle with supporting context
- Use visual cues (bold, color, icons) to rescue important middle-position items from the serial position penalty
- Limit list length to 7 or fewer items where recall matters; beyond that, consider grouping with subheadings

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
  - **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Findability score
  - **Efficiency**: Time on task, Error counts
  - **Engagement & Usage**: Clickthrough rate, Bounce rate
  **How to measure**: Use first-click testing and eye-tracking to verify that critical items in primacy and recency positions receive the most attention and action. A/B test navigation and list layouts with critical items repositioned to first/last positions and measure clickthrough and task success rates.
