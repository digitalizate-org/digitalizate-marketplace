## Why
Tab labels are navigation: they must give users enough information to predict what's in each tab before clicking. Generic or overly long labels defeat tabs as a quick navigation mechanism. Labels of more than 2–3 words usually indicate that the tab groupings haven't been thought through properly — if you can't describe a tab's content in 1–2 nouns, the grouping may need refinement. Labels must distinguish tabs clearly from each other with no overlapping meaning.

## How to implement
- Keep labels to 1–2 words; longer labels suggest tabs may not be the right pattern.
- Use strong, noun-led descriptive language ("Alerts," "Configuration," "Activity Log").
- Use sentence- or title-case consistently; avoid ALL CAPS.
- Ensure labels distinguish each tab clearly from others — no overlapping meaning.
- Test by asking users to predict content before clicking — if they consistently guess wrong, revise the label or the grouping.

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Findability score
- **Efficiency**: Time on task, Error counts
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: Ease-of-use score, CSAT
**How to measure**: Conduct label comprehension tests asking users to predict tab content before clicking; measure first-click accuracy and task success rate in usability sessions; track tab-switching frequency as a proxy for label clarity — high switching suggests users are not finding content where expected.
