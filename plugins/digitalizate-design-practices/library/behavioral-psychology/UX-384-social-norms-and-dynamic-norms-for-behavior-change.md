## Why
Social norm theory establishes that people adjust their behavior to align with what they perceive as normal or typical in their reference group — a phenomenon Cialdini calls 'descriptive norms.' In product design, communicating what most similar users do is a powerful behavioral prompt: 'Most teams in your industry use weekly check-ins' or 'Users like you typically complete setup in 15 minutes.' Coglode's Dynamic Norms research extends this: communicating that a behavior is becoming more common ('An increasing number of companies are adopting X') is more persuasive for behavior change than citing an already-established norm, because it creates the impression of a tipping point in progress.

## How to implement
- Display peer-comparison data in user dashboards: 'You're in the top 20% of users for feature adoption this month'
- Use social norms in onboarding prompts: 'Teams with your setup typically complete their first project in week 1 — you're on track'
- Apply dynamic norms to feature adoption messaging: 'More teams are switching to automated reporting — see how it works'
- Show industry benchmarks to contextualize user performance and create aspiration toward the norm: '75% of companies your size use X feature'
- Use descriptive norms in empty states: 'Most users start by connecting their calendar — it takes 2 minutes'
- Avoid injunctive norms (what people should do) alone — combine with descriptive norms (what people actually do) for maximum effect

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Engagement & Usage**: Active users, Feature usage
- **Adoption & Retention**: Returning visitors, Renewed subscriptions
- **Revenue**: Conversion rate
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: Satisfaction rating, Confidence rating
**How to measure**: A/B test norm-based messaging ("Most users in your industry do X") against standard CTAs; measure behavior change rate, feature activation rate, and retention cohort differences to assess the influence of dynamic norm framing.
