## Why
Kahneman and Tversky's Prospect Theory established that losses loom roughly twice as large as equivalent gains in psychological impact — losing $100 feels worse than gaining $100 feels good. This asymmetry has profound implications for how designers should frame upgrade offers, trial endings, and feature limitations. Framing a premium feature in terms of what the user will miss out on ('You're missing advanced analytics — your competitors aren't') is more motivating than framing it as a gain. Nielsen Norman Group recommends framing decisions to highlight potential losses at the moment of decision, especially for insurance, security, and premium features.

## How to implement
- Frame trial expiration messages as loss: 'You'll lose access to your saved projects in 3 days' rather than 'Upgrade to keep your projects'
- On pricing pages, show what's NOT included in lower tiers rather than only what IS included in higher ones
- Use loss framing for onboarding urgency: 'Don't lose your 30-day streak' performs better than 'Keep your streak'
- When displaying warnings (security risks, compliance gaps), quantify the potential downside in concrete terms
- For feature paywalls, show a greyed-out preview of locked features rather than hiding them entirely — users want what they almost have
- Balance loss framing with reassurance (guarantees, reversibility) to avoid anxiety that paralyzes rather than motivates

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
  - **Revenue**: Conversion rate, Revenue per session, Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)
  - **Adoption & Retention**: Renewed subscriptions
  - **Satisfaction & Perception**: CSAT, NPS
  **How to measure**: A/B test loss-framed vs. gain-framed messaging on upgrade and warning prompts, measuring conversion rate and downstream churn rate. Use qualitative post-interaction surveys to ensure loss framing does not create negative brand perception that offsets conversion gains.
