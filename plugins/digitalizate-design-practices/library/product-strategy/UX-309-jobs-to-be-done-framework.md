## Why
Frame product requirements around what users "hire" a product to do — the job they need accomplished in a specific context — rather than around features or user characteristics. JTBD shifts focus from "who the user is" to "what they're trying to accomplish and why." For enterprise software, JTBD surfaces that the real job often isn't "create a report" but "give my manager confidence that the numbers are correct" — a far richer design target that shapes not just the report feature but its sharing, annotation, and accuracy-signaling capabilities.

## How to implement
- For each significant user request, apply the JTBD format: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Identify functional, social, and emotional jobs separately — enterprise users have all three.
- Design solutions to satisfy the job, not just implement the requested feature.
- Conduct 30–60 minute JTBD interviews focused on the context and timeline of users' decisions, not their opinions about specific features.

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Completion rate
- **Adoption & Retention**: Account creation rate, Returning visitors, Renewed subscriptions
- **Revenue**: Conversion rate, Customer Lifetime Value (CLV)
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: CSAT, NPS
**How to measure**: Map product features to specific JTBD and track whether task success rates and conversion rates improve for user journeys redesigned around a job. Use NPS and CSAT surveys segmented by user job-to-be-done to verify that satisfaction is higher for cohorts whose primary job is well-supported.
