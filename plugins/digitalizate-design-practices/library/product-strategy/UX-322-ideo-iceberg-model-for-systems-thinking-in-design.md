## Why
Systems thinking in design recognizes that user behavior, product performance, and organizational outcomes are not the product of isolated decisions or individual features, but of interconnected structures, feedback loops, and mental models operating beneath what is immediately visible. The iceberg metaphor, popularized in the systems thinking tradition and adopted by IDEO's strategic design practice, distinguishes between the visible tip — observable events and symptoms — and the submerged mass: patterns of behavior, underlying structures, and the mental models that perpetuate those structures. In product strategy, this distinction is critical because most design interventions target the tip. Bug fixes, UI improvements, and onboarding patches treat symptoms; they rarely address the structural conditions that produced the problem in the first place.
IDEO's application of the iceberg model to design strategy directs teams to diagnose at the structural level before prescribing at the surface level. A recurring support ticket about user confusion in a workflow is an event; the pattern reveals that users consistently misunderstand the relationship between two product concepts; the structure is a data model that conflates two distinct entities into one ambiguous UI state; the mental model sustaining it is an engineering-first assumption that users will learn the underlying data schema. Intervening only at the event level (better error messages) leaves the structural cause intact. Intervening at the structural level (separating the entities in the data model and UI) resolves the class of problems permanently. This is the intellectual foundation for IDEO's emphasis on "root cause" framing in design briefs.
For product strategy practice, the iceberg model serves a second function: scoping. When initiating a new product initiative, applying systems thinking surfaces the structural constraints and mental model assumptions that will determine whether the intervention succeeds or creates new problems downstream. Teams that skip this analysis — proceeding directly from symptom to solution — frequently discover that their well-designed feature has unintended consequences on adjacent systems. The model disciplines the scoping conversation by requiring explicit mapping of the problem space at multiple levels of abstraction before solution development begins, reducing the risk of solving the wrong problem elegantly.

## How to implement
- Apply systems thinking to identify root causes: what pattern, structure, or mental model is producing the recurring problem?
- Map the system: document the visible events (surface), then identify the patterns beneath, the underlying structures driving those patterns, and the mental models sustaining those structures.
- Use the iceberg model to frame design briefs: start with 'What's the visible symptom?' then work down to 'What structure in the product creates this pattern?'
- Focus design interventions at the structural level for lasting change, not just surface-level symptom relief.
- Facilitate stakeholder workshops that move conversation from 'events' to 'patterns' to 'structures' to avoid repeated surface-level fixes.

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Completion rate
- **Efficiency**: Support tickets reduction, Training time
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: NPS, CSAT
**How to measure**: Evaluate whether systemic design interventions (e.g., fixing underlying data model vs. UI patch) reduce support ticket volume more durably than surface-level fixes. Use NPS tracking over 6–12 months to assess satisfaction trends tied to systemic improvements versus cosmetic changes.
