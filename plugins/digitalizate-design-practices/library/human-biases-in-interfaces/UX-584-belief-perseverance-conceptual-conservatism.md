## Why
Belief perseverance, or conceptual conservatism, is the tendency for beliefs to persist after the evidence that originally formed them has been clearly and explicitly discredited. The foundational research by Ross, Lepper, and Hubbard (1975) used a 'debriefing paradigm' in which participants formed beliefs based on fabricated performance feedback, then received a complete and unambiguous debriefing that the feedback was false. Despite this explicit correction, participants' beliefs remained significantly higher than control groups who never received the false feedback — demonstrating that belief formation triggers the construction of self-sustaining justificatory networks that survive the removal of the original evidence. The belief becomes self-supporting through a web of associated reasoning that was built after, not before, the initial formation.
Belief perseverance is distinct from confirmation bias, which involves selectively seeking confirming evidence, in that it involves maintaining beliefs even when confronted with clear disconfirmation. The underlying mechanism is explanatory: when a person forms a belief, they construct explanatory narratives that support it — 'I believe X because Y and Z.' Even after X's original basis is removed, Y and Z remain as independent supports. To eliminate the belief, one must not only remove the original foundation but also systematically address each of the secondary justifications that were constructed afterward, which is a significantly more demanding cognitive task.
In product teams, belief perseverance operates with particular force in two contexts. First, early-stage product beliefs formed during founding or initial research persist long after the product and its user base have fundamentally changed. A team that concluded early users were 'technically sophisticated early adopters' will continue designing for that persona years after the product has reached a mainstream audience, even when presented with contradicting user research — because the belief has accumulated years of supporting rationalizations. Second, professional expertise beliefs — 'users don't scroll,' 'above the fold is all that matters,' 'users read error messages carefully' — persist in team practice as zombie conventions long after the empirical data has shifted, because they are supported by the professional identities and prior successful decisions of senior team members.

## How to implement
- Maintain a 'beliefs and evidence' register: document each major product/design belief and its current evidence base; flag beliefs whose evidence is older than 18 months
- After every A/B test and research study, explicitly document which beliefs were confirmed, which were disconfirmed, and formally update the team's working assumptions
- Conduct annual 'first principles reviews': strip away accumulated beliefs and re-examine core product assumptions from fresh data
- When onboarding new team members, expose them to the evidence for major product beliefs—not just the conclusions—so they can independently evaluate rather than inherit beliefs
- For critical design decisions, require 'belief dating': when was this belief formed? On what evidence? Is that evidence still current?
- Create a 'zombie beliefs' list: design decisions or beliefs that have been officially discredited but continue to influence team decisions

## Metrics
Understanding and designing for this bias can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Error rate, Task success rate
- **Adoption & Retention**: Returning visitors
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: CSAT, Brand perception
**How to measure**: Track user adoption rates for new features or workflows that contradict established conventions, measuring error rates and negative feedback volume over the introduction period. Use surveys and NPS tracking to detect resistance to evidence-based design changes, and test incremental change-messaging approaches to measure which framing reduces the Semmelweis reflex response.
