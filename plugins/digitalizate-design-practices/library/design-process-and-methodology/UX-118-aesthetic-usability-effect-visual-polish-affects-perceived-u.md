## Why
Users often perceive aesthetically pleasing design as more usable, even when actual usability is equivalent. Research by Kurosu and Kashimura (1995) found users rated aesthetically appealing ATM interfaces as easier to use than functionally identical alternatives. Importantly, this effect can mask real usability problems in testing — recruit skeptical users and test with task completion metrics, not satisfaction ratings alone. Visual polish buys tolerance: a professional-looking interface makes a 3-second wait feel acceptable where an ugly one makes it intolerable.

## How to implement
- Invest in visual polish — consistent spacing, a clean type scale, a coherent color palette — even in data-dense UIs.
- Use the aesthetic effect strategically at high-stakes moments: onboarding, dashboards, externally shared reports.
- Be aware it masks usability problems — supplement preference testing with task-completion metrics.
- Apply it to loading screens and progress states: a well-designed loading animation makes waits feel shorter.
- Don't let it become a reason to avoid usability fixes.

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: Ease-of-use score, Brand perception, CSAT
- **Adoption & Retention**: Returning visitors, Trial requests
- **Engagement & Usage**: Sessions per user, Abandonment rate
**How to measure**: Gather perceived usability ratings (e.g., SUS or UMUX-Lite) before and after visual redesign work to isolate the aesthetic-usability effect. Pair with A/B testing on returning visitor rates and session frequency to assess whether improved visual polish drives retention.
