## Why
Group decision-making is systematically impaired by well-documented cognitive and social failure modes that, without explicit countermeasures, reliably degrade both the quality of design decisions and team cohesion. Groupthink — the tendency for cohesive groups to suppress dissenting opinions in favor of consensus — is particularly destructive in design reviews where senior stakeholder opinions anchor early discussion and implicitly discourage challenge. Research by Janis (1972) first formalized groupthink as a causal factor in high-profile organizational failures, and subsequent work by Sunstein and Hastie in *Wiser: Getting Beyond Groupthink to Make Groups Smarter* (2015) demonstrated that deliberative structures like pre-mortem analysis, anonymous opinion elicitation, and designated devil's advocacy reduce the frequency of groupthink-driven decisions by 40–70% in experimental settings. In product design contexts, these failure modes manifest as HiPPO bias (Highest Paid Person's Opinion), vocal convergence in design critiques, and premature closure on the first plausible design direction.
Structured decision processes counteract these biases by separating ideation from evaluation, making individual positions legible before group discussion begins, and assigning procedural roles that legitimize dissent. The DACI framework (Driver, Approver, Contributor, Informed) clarifies accountability and reduces the ambiguity that allows HiPPO bias to fill the vacuum — when decision roles are explicit, participants orient to their legitimate contribution rather than deferring to seniority. Dot voting, silent brainstorming, and async written feedback before synchronous meetings create conditions where more perspectives surface before social dynamics suppress them. Nielsen Norman Group research on design critique effectiveness consistently identifies lack of structured process as the primary cause of critique sessions that produce vague feedback, personality-driven conflict, and unchanged designs.
For product teams running design reviews, the operational implication is that meeting structure is as important as meeting content. Practitioners should default to formats that include a silent review period (2–3 minutes of individual notes before discussion), explicit separation of clarifying questions from evaluative feedback, and documented decision records that capture not just the outcome but the alternatives considered and the rationale for rejection. Teams that implement these practices report faster convergence on higher-quality decisions and significantly lower meeting frustration — because the process, not seniority or volume, determines the outcome.

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
- **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Completion rate
- **Efficiency**: Time on task, Support tickets reduction
- **Satisfaction & Perception**: CSAT, NPS
**How to measure**: Track decision cycle time (time from brief to approved design) and the number of design revision rounds as process efficiency metrics. Correlate with downstream usability test scores and support ticket volume to assess whether structured decision-making produces better-quality designs with fewer post-launch corrections.
