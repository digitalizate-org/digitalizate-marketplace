## Why
Design products to help users achieve their personal and professional goals (end conditions and motivations) rather than merely to complete tasks (activities and procedures). Goals are stable over time; tasks evolve as technology changes. A goal is "process invoices without errors"; a task is "click the Approve button." Designing for goals produces interfaces that remain relevant as workflows evolve, while designing for tasks produces UIs that become outdated as processes change. For complex enterprise software, this distinction prevents the perpetual feature-chasing that comes from optimizing for task completion rather than user success.

## How to implement
- For each primary persona, define their Experience Goals (feel competent, in control), End Goals (accomplish the work outcome), and Life Goals (professional identity and aspiration).
- Evaluate every design decision against whether it serves these goals or merely exposes system internals.
- When writing requirements, use goal-language ("user can confidently finalize the monthly close") rather than task-language ("user can click Submit on the close screen").

## Metrics
Applying this pattern can positively influence the following UX metrics:
  - **Effectiveness**: Task success rate, Completion rate
  - **Satisfaction & Perception**: CSAT, NPS, Ease-of-use score
  - **Adoption & Retention**: Returning visitors, Renewed subscriptions
  **How to measure**: Use post-task surveys to assess whether users feel the product helped them achieve their broader goals, not just individual tasks. Compare NPS scores for products designed around explicit user goals vs. those designed around feature checklists, using retrospective design audits.
